How to rotate a video that was taken sideways

Almost nobody actually films sideways. The camera stores the picture one way round and writes down how you were holding the phone, and then two pieces of software disagree about that note. Here is what is really happening, how to turn the clip the right way up so every player agrees, and how to stop it happening on the next one.

The cause

Why it looks sideways on one device and fine on another

Your phone did not record the picture crooked. It recorded the picture, and separately recorded a note saying which way up you were holding it.

A phone's camera sensor is fixed in the body of the handset, so the raw picture comes off it the same way round no matter how you hold the phone. Rather than rearranging millions of pixels while recording, the phone stores the picture as the sensor saw it and adds a single rotation value to the file — 90°, 180° or 270°. Every player is expected to read that value and turn the picture before drawing it.

Most do. Some do not, and that is the entire problem:

Usually reads the rotation flag Often ignores it
The phone that filmed it Older desktop video editors
Modern desktop players Projectors and some smart TVs
Web browsers Media players built into older hardware
Major social platforms Upload and conversion pipelines that re-encode
Nothing is broken or corrupted.

The file is completely valid. Two programs simply disagree about one number in its header. That is also why the problem seems to come and go — it depends entirely on what you happen to open the clip in.

Step one

Which way does it need to turn?

Worth thirty seconds, because turning it the wrong way and back costs two encodes instead of one. Look at where the top of the real-world scene — sky, ceiling, people's heads — has ended up.

Top of the scene is… Turn it
On the left of the screen Right, clockwise, 90°
On the right of the screen Left, anticlockwise, 90°
At the bottom 180° — two quarter turns, saved once

If you are not sure, previewing beats guessing. ClipTurn's Rotate Video tab turns the preview without touching the file, so you can cycle through the directions until the clip looks right and only then save.

Step two

Fix it permanently on Windows

“Permanently” means turning the stored picture, not the note about it. Then there is no flag left for anything to ignore.

  1. Copy anything irreplaceable first

    Rotation writes a new file over the old one. For a wedding, a first birthday or anything else you cannot film twice, put a copy somewhere else before you start. This applies to every rotation tool, not just this one.

  2. Install ClipTurn and open it once

    It is free from Microsoft Store and needs no account. Opening it once lets Windows register its File Explorer commands.

  3. Right-click the clip and choose a direction

    Open ClipTurn – Video Rotator and pick Rotate video left or Rotate video right. Select several clips first and the same turn is applied to each of them independently.

  4. Let it check its own work

    The turned picture is built as a separate file and fully verified — picture, audio, tracks and the metadata it promised to keep — before it is allowed to replace anything. If it cannot prove a faithful result, it stops and leaves your original alone.

Prefer to preview and keep a backup?

Choose Open in ClipTurn from the same right-click menu instead. That loads the clip in the app, where you can preview each direction before committing, and switch on Create a backup before replacement in Settings so the original is kept beside the result. Explorer rotations deliberately never keep a persistent backup.

The alternative

The lossless shortcut, and its catch

There is a second way to fix a sideways clip: leave the picture alone and rewrite the rotation flag so it says something different. It is instant and costs no quality whatsoever, because not a single pixel is re-encoded.

  • It only exists for MP4, M4V and MOV files — the containers that carry that flag.
  • Any player, editor or website that ignored the old flag will ignore the new one, so the clip can still turn up sideways in exactly the place that caused the problem.
  • It is the right choice when the video only has to look correct in modern software and you refuse to spend any quality at all.

ClipTurn offers this as a reviewed rotated copy when it applies: the choice is shown to you with its exact trade-offs listed, the copy is saved under a new name, and the original is never replaced without approval. If the sideways clip is destined for something old or unpredictable, physical rotation is the safer answer. The MP4 guide compares both routes properly.

Step three

Check the fix actually held

The most common false victory is a player that has quietly remembered a rotation setting. Two checks settle it in under a minute.

  1. Open it somewhere else

    Use a different program from the one you fixed it in — drag the file into a browser window, or open it in Films & TV. A genuinely rotated clip looks right everywhere.

  2. Look at the dimensions

    Right-click the file, open Properties → Details and read the frame width and height. After a real quarter turn, 1920×1080 becomes 1080×1920. If they are unchanged, the picture was not turned — only a flag was, or nothing was.

At volume

A whole holiday's worth of clips

Sideways footage rarely arrives alone. If the phone was held the same way all afternoon, every clip from that afternoon needs the same quarter turn.

  • Select them all in File Explorer and rotate once — the direction applies to each file separately, so a problem with one does not affect the rest.
  • For a folder, or for a mix that needs sorting first, drop it onto ClipTurn's Batch Rotate tab. Every row keeps its own status and reason.
  • One direction applies to a whole batch run. Clips that need the opposite turn belong in a second run, not the same one.

Next time

Stopping it happening again

  • Settle before you record, not after. The orientation is decided when recording starts. Turning the phone once the clip is running changes nothing about that clip.
  • Unlock rotation first. With rotation lock on, the phone can commit to the wrong orientation before you have noticed.
  • Do not start filming with the phone flat. Held parallel to the ground, the sensors cannot tell which way up you mean to be, so the phone guesses — and that guess is what gets written into the file.
  • Watch for the switch mid-clip. Starting upright and rolling to landscape while recording gives you a clip whose flag matches the beginning and not the rest.

Answers

Common questions

Why was my video recorded sideways?

It was not, strictly speaking. Phone camera sensors have a fixed orientation, so the picture is nearly always stored the same way round regardless of how you held the phone. The phone records how it was tilted as a rotation flag in the file. Players that read the flag turn the picture for you; players that ignore it show the stored picture, which looks sideways.

Why does my video look fine on my phone but sideways on my computer?

The phone wrote a rotation flag and reads it back, so it always looks right there. Some desktop software, older editors, projectors, televisions and upload processors ignore that flag and display the raw stored picture instead. Nothing is corrupted — two pieces of software simply disagree about a single value.

How do I permanently rotate a sideways video?

Rotate the stored picture rather than the flag. On Windows, install ClipTurn from Microsoft Store, right-click the video in File Explorer and choose Rotate video left or Rotate video right. The saved file carries its new orientation everywhere, because there is no longer a flag for anything to ignore.

Will rotating a sideways video lose quality?

Physically turning compressed video means decoding and encoding it once more, which costs a single generation of quality that you are very unlikely to see. Rotating the same clip repeatedly does accumulate loss, so it is worth checking the direction before you save rather than turning it back and forth.

How do I stop my phone recording sideways videos?

Unlock rotation before you start filming rather than after, and let the camera app settle in the orientation you want before pressing record. Rotating the phone mid-recording does not change the orientation of a clip that has already started. Avoid holding the phone flat while opening the camera, because the app cannot tell which way up you intend to be.
